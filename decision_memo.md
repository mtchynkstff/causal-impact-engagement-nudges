## Executive Summary

We evaluated the causal impact of a targeted engagement nudge intervention on student behavior using a randomized experimental design and a difference-in-differences (DiD) framework. The intervention increased student engagement by an estimated **0.37 additional active days per week** (95% CI: [0.32, 0.42]), representing an **~11% increase** relative to baseline engagement levels.

Effects were substantially larger for students with low baseline engagement, who experienced an estimated **0.54 additional active days per week**, compared to **0.30 days** for higher-engagement students. Over the six-week post-intervention period, this translates to approximately **2.2 additional active days per student overall**, and **3.2 additional days** for low-engagement students.

**Recommendation:** The intervention should be deployed in a **targeted manner**, prioritizing lower-engagement students, where impact is highest and marginal returns are greatest.

---

## Context & Evaluation Design

The intervention consisted of personalized engagement nudges delivered during the academic term. Students were randomly assigned to treatment and control groups using stratified randomization to ensure balance across grade levels and socioeconomic bands.

To estimate causal impact, we applied a **difference-in-differences (DiD)** approach comparing pre- and post-intervention changes in the treatment group against the same changes in the control group. Validation checks confirmed balanced baselines, parallel pre-intervention trends, and no detectable placebo effects prior to rollout.

---

## Key Findings

### Overall Impact

- **Estimated effect:** +**0.37 active days per week**
- **95% confidence interval:** [0.32, 0.42]
- **Relative lift:** ~**11%** compared to the control group’s pre-intervention baseline (3.32 days/week)

This indicates a modest but reliable behavioral shift rather than a transient or noisy effect.

---

### Heterogeneous Effects by Baseline Engagement

Impact varied meaningfully by baseline engagement level:

| Student Group | Estimated Weekly Gain |
|--------------|----------------------|
| Higher baseline engagement | +0.30 days |
| Lower baseline engagement | **+0.54 days** |

Lower-engagement students benefited nearly **80% more** than their higher-engagement peers, suggesting diminishing returns among already-engaged users.

---

### Cumulative Impact

When expressed cumulatively over the six-week post-intervention period:

- **Average student:** ~**2.2 additional active days**
- **Low-engagement student:** ~**3.2 additional active days**

These gains are likely to be meaningful for retention and habit formation, particularly among students at risk of disengagement.

---

## Robustness & Confidence

Results are stable across multiple specifications:

- Aggregating outcomes to the student level (pre vs. post averages) yields a similar directional estimate
- Pre-period trend tests show no evidence of differential trends prior to the intervention
- A placebo intervention applied during the pre-period produces no treatment-specific effect

Together, these checks increase confidence that observed effects are attributable to the intervention rather than underlying trends or noise.

---

## Limitations

- The analysis focuses on short-term engagement outcomes; longer-term academic or retention effects may differ.
- Results reflect average effects within the evaluation window and may attenuate over time without reinforcement.
- The evaluation does not incorporate cost data; conclusions are based on effectiveness rather than cost-effectiveness.

---

## Recommendation

Based on the observed effect sizes and heterogeneity, we recommend:

1. **Targeted deployment** of engagement nudges to lower-engagement students
2. Avoiding blanket rollout, which is likely to yield diminishing marginal returns
3. Continued measurement to assess persistence of effects over longer horizons

This approach maximizes impact while maintaining efficiency and aligns intervention intensity with demonstrated need.
